Why Daybook exists

The questions come at 3am. By the appointment, they've gone.

When I was told I had stage 4 pancreatic cancer, already in my liver and my spine, everything became a blur. Tablets at different times. Temperatures. Side effects. What I'd managed to eat. And the questions, always in the middle of the night.

Some went on scraps of paper. Most went nowhere. I'd sit down in front of the oncologist with ten minutes and not know where to start.

Mark Brown, who made Daybook

How it started

I believed in the doctors. Shelley did the research. Together it got easier.

Shelley kept the list. I built a simple tracker so neither of us had to remember. Medicines and when they were taken. Temperature, blood pressure, weight. What I ate and drank. How the day felt, morning and evening. And every question, the moment it came to us.

It grew into Daybook: one place, shared between the two of us, so nothing depended on either of us remembering.

What you do each day

  • Two check-ins, morning and evening, a minute each.
  • Tap to log a dose, a temperature, a drink, a meal, a symptom, a pain, a bowel movement.
  • Talk instead of type. Tap the microphone under any note and say it. Keyboards are hard on a bad day.
  • Ask the team. Write the question when it comes to you. It waits for the appointment, and the answer is recorded against it.

The report

Write it down. Hand it over.

Turn up with two pages the team can read in a minute.

Before an appointment, Daybook gathers everything since the last one into a report: the questions you want to ask, a summary of what changed, the charts, and the notes from each week. Print it and take it with you, or email it to the nurse, the dietitian or the oncologist beforehand. It leaves room for them to write.

No more trying to remember. No more scraps of paper. Just the facts, in the order they'll ask.

What the report holds

  • Your questions, with space for the answers.
  • A summary of anything worth a mention: a high temperature, pain creeping up, nights of poor sleep, weight going down.
  • The charts: temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen, weight, sleep, pain and mood.
  • The notes, week by week, in your own words.

The people around you

Your circle sees what you see. Nobody has to ask twice.

Invite the people who help: a partner, a daughter, a friend who drives you to chemo. They see the same record and the same report, from their own phone. The ones who want to know can keep up without you having to repeat it, and the ones you'd rather talk to about anything else can.

Daybook was built for cancer treatment, because that's where we lived it. Then friends started asking for it: for Crohn's and colitis, for the menopause, for anything with a team you see every few weeks and a lot of days in between. So it's set up for those too. Private by design.

Yours, and only yours.

Each circle's records are seen only by its own members. Nobody else, and nothing sold to anyone.

Kept in the UK.

Your records are stored in London, over a secure connection.

Nothing leaves without a tap.

A report goes to the team only when you send it. The AI helper reads a letter only when you ask it to.

Not medical advice.

Daybook keeps the record and does the sums. Your team makes the decisions. In an emergency, call 999 or 111.
