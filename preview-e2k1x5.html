Why Daybook exists

The questions come when they come. By the appointment, they've gone.

In August 2026 I was told I had stage 4 pancreatic cancer, already in my liver and my spine. I was 44. I was given 6 to 12 months, depending on how I responded to treatment.

After that, everything became a blur. News like that takes your memory with it.

Then came the everyday part. Tablets at different times. Temperatures. Side effects. What I'd managed to eat. And the questions, which arrived whenever they liked: in the car, over dinner, lying awake.

Some went on scraps of paper. Most went nowhere. I'd forget to tell Shelley what I wanted to ask. By the time I sat down in front of the oncologist, I had very little to say and no idea where to start.

Mark Brown, creator of Daybook

How it started

The worst news of our lives. So we made something with it.

Daybook wasn't planned. It grew one problem at a time.

  1. Medicines

    It started as a way to track my tablets. I still forgot them, so I added Shelley. Her phone gets my reminders too, so she can tell me to take them, or fetch them for me.

  2. Pain

    Then new pains started. I wanted to ask the nurses and doctors what was causing them, but I'd forget, so I logged them as they happened.

  3. Questions

    Then we realised Shelley could add her own questions, the ones that came out of our conversations. I've always been dreadful at admin. Shelley has always been the best administrator I know; even our friends ask her to sort theirs. That's when I could see the beginning of Daybook.

  4. Food

    I had to put on weight before treatment, and Shelley kept asking what I'd eaten. A food diary made it easy to answer.

  5. Check-ins

    As treatment went on, I met new people, like the pain consultant. Morning and evening check-ins gave us a proper way to talk about my pain.

  6. Mood

    I started therapy, so I began tracking my mood and wellbeing. I'm good at telling myself I'm fine when I'm not, so Shelley can score how she thinks I am too.

  7. Reports

    With all of that in one place, it made sense to summarise it. Before each appointment I send it to my nurses and doctors, or print it and take it in: my readings, my week and, most importantly, every question we'd collected.

Then friends with Crohn's asked for it, and it grew again.

Daybook doesn't give medical advice, and never will. It's a personal medical journal: what happened, in our own words, kept for the people who need to see it.

Shelley

None of this works without her.

I have the ideas. Shelley is my sounding board, and the dedicated grown-up who gets me thinking.

We've been married 22 years. This news hit her as hard as it hit me. We were supposed to grow old together.

Daybook is the good thing we made out of the worst news, and we made it together.

Then Helen asked

This could be life changing.

I was round Mark's when he showed me the Daybook he'd created to help him keep track of his cancer. I was blown away. I have Crohn's disease, and straight away I asked whether it could work for that too.

Before Daybook, I'd get myself in a right old pickle trying to remember everything, then come home from an appointment and think, "Oh, I should have asked that."

Now I can log how I've slept, what I've had to drink, even a mild headache, and start joining the dots. On the days I meditate, go to the gym or use the hot and cold tub, my symptoms often seem less troublesome, and I've got the record to show my team. My questions are there in front of me too.

For me, having everything in one space is a game changer.

Helen, living with Crohn's disease

Between appointments

The weeks in between are where it all happens. Daybook is built for them.

A minute in the morning, a minute at night, a few taps through the day. By the next appointment, the whole story is there.

Two check-ins a day

A minute each, morning and evening: sleep, symptoms, pain and mood, one question a screen. Say it instead of typing it with Tap to speak.

Log anything in a few taps

Vitals, pain, a drink, a meal, a note, sleep, bowels, a symptom, a dose: nine tiles on Today, each a few taps, with the time filled in for you.

Medicines and reminders

Every medicine with its dose and how many are done today. Log now is one tap. Reminders arrive as notifications at the times you set, with the names kept off the lock screen if you like.

Questions that wait for the appointment

Write it when it comes to you, say who it is for, and it heads that team's report with room under it for the answer. Record the answer in clinic so nobody has to take notes.

Your week, in your own words

Five minutes once a week, ending on the day you choose, your treatment day if you like: what helped, what was hardest, the week in a sentence. The mood and wellbeing report is built from it.

Who to contact, one tap

The ward, the nurse, the GP, the advice line: a name, a number, an email. Call or email in one tap, and send any report to any of them from the app.

Talk instead of type

Every box has a Tap to speak button under it. Tap, say it, tap again. The words appear and can be changed like typed text. Keyboards are hard on a bad day.

Letters explained

Photograph a clinic letter, a label or a plan and Daybook Assistant explains it in plain English, only when you ask, with the questions worth taking back to the team.

Apple Health, if you want it

Steps, sleep, workouts, weight and your food app's totals can arrive by themselves. Everything can be typed in instead, so Daybook works in full without it.

Crohn's or colitis

Bowels with blood and urgency, a flare plan in the team's own words, "I think I'm flaring" with a 7-day summary to read out, results as the lab gave them, injections and steroid courses on the calendar.

Cancer treatment

Cycles as the team counts them, sessions marked done, side effects asked in the check-ins for three days after each one, and the alert card's limits on every temperature.

Your care circle

Invite the people who help. They see what you see, from their own phone. A carer adds their own view of the day and the week, kept apart from yours, never mixed in.

Step 1

Loading the walk-through.

    The reports

    Write it down. Hand it over.

    Before an appointment, Daybook gathers everything since the last one into a few pages the team can read in a minute, with room for them to write. Print them, or send them to the nurse, the dietitian or the consultant from the app.

    Every report says where it came from

    • Your questions, with space for the answers, and the answers recorded in clinic.
    • What changed, in plain sentences: a high temperature, pain creeping up, nights of poor sleep, weight going down. Facts the app worked out, never advice.
    • The charts, drawn from what was logged, with the limits your team uses shaded in.
    • The notes, week by week, in your own words, or summarised by Daybook Assistant if you ask it to.
    • Sent from the app, as a PDF, to any contact with an email address, with a copy to you.

    The people around you

    Your circle sees what you see. Nobody has to ask twice.

    Invite the people who help: a partner, a daughter, a friend who drives you to chemo. They see the same record and the same report, from their own phone. The ones who want to know can keep up without you having to repeat it, and the ones you'd rather talk to about anything else can.

    The circle is yours. Only you, or whoever set it up for you, can invite or remove anyone, and nobody outside it, Mark included, ever looks.

    "I can keep track of what's happening with Mark without us having the same cancer conversation every time, so when we're together we can talk about other things. But there's always enough in the app that I can see when they might need extra help, and I can just ask Shelley what I need to do."

    Hayley, Mark and Shelley's friend

    Private by design.

    Yours, and only yours.

    Each circle's records are seen only by its own members, enforced by the database itself. Nobody else, and nothing sold to anyone.
